# 001 — Grace Hopper

**Cluster:** C1 — Foundations of intent capture & specification
**Roster role:** First compilers (A-0, FLOW-MATIC); machine-independent programming
**SpecForge anchors:** README.md vision ("intent is compiled, not interpreted"), docs/spec-writing-flow.md

## Why this engineer
Hopper invented the founding move SpecForge repeats for intent: in "The Education of a Computer" (1952) she argued that rote translation should be delegated to the machine, so people write what they mean and the compiler does the rest. FLOW-MATIC proved a language can look like human language — English keywords in, validated program out — which is exactly the `.spec` reading experience. Her drive toward a *common*, machine-checked business vocabulary (COBOL) is the ancestor of the Graph Protocol as an open standard every consumer can trust.

## References for SpecForge
**Key works**
- **The Education of a Computer** — ACM National Meeting (Pittsburgh), 1952; reprinted in *Annals of the History of Computing* 9(3), 1986. The founding argument that translation from human intent to machine form is a compilation, not a chore.
- **FLOW-MATIC Programming System** — Remington-Rand UNIVAC manual, 1958. First English-like data-processing language; direct precursor of declarative, keyword-block spec DSLs.
- **COBOL: Initial Specifications for a Common Business Oriented Language** — CODASYL, U.S. Dept. of Defense, 1960. Precedent for a shared, vendor-neutral vocabulary validated by every implementation — SpecForge's H3 "the standard" horizon.
- Kurt W. Beyer, *Grace Hopper and the Invention of the Information Age* — MIT Press, 2009. Best account of her compiler-first philosophy and how openness beat proprietary lock-in.
